# How these assessments are built

The design constraints below are what separate an assessment that predicts job
performance from one that just feels rigorous.

## The four-stage spine

Every assessment uses the same structure. The spine is what makes this a series
rather than a pile of one-off case studies.

| Stage | What it is | What it surfaces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Diagnose | A pile of mixed signals, one of which is buried and binding | Whether they separate symptom from cause |
| 2. Allocate | A fixed budget of time or money against a costed menu | Whether they sequence, or just work hard |
| 3. Curveball | New information that invalidates the obvious plan | Whether they update or defend |
| 4. Artifact | Produce the actual thing the job produces | Whether they can do the job |

Stage 3 is the one most assessments skip, and it is the most predictive. A
one-shot scenario tests judgment at a single point. Watching someone abandon a
plan they just committed to tests something much closer to what the job is
actually like.

Stage 4 matters because it is the only stage that is a work sample rather than a
description of work. Someone can reason well about a renewal and still write an
email that gets deleted.

## Design rules for the scenario itself

**Bury exactly one binding constraint.** In the CSM assessment it is the 60-day
notice clause against a 67-day renewal — the real deadline is 7 days away, and
nothing says so. It has to be derivable from what is on the page and invisible to
someone skimming. One per assessment. Two makes it a puzzle hunt.

**Make the loudest signal the wrong one.** The CSM's usage drop and the GTM's
flat signups are the things a weak candidate will fix. Both are downstream
symptoms. The gap between what is loud and what is load-bearing is where the
scoring signal lives.

**Cost every option in stage 2, and include one that does not fit the budget.**
The onsite QBR costs 8 hours against a 6-hour budget. Selecting it is not a
judgment error, it is a reading error, and you want to know.

**Make the curveball reframe the problem, not just complicate it.** Priya's call
converts a satisfaction problem into a competitive displacement under a budget
mandate. New facts that only add difficulty test stamina. New facts that change
the category test judgment.

**Give the curveball an opening the buyer handed you.** Marcus's "reduce tool
sprawl" is his own stated goal and the route to keeping Meridian. The strongest
candidates argue the customer's agenda back to them. This is the single most
discriminating moment in the CSM assessment.

## Scoring rules

**Anchor every level in observable behavior.** "Shows strong judgment" is not a
rubric. "Identifies the 7-day notice deadline as the binding constraint and the
sponsor vacuum as the root cause" is. If two reviewers can read the same answer
and disagree about the level, the anchor is not concrete enough.

**Score the profile, not the total.** A 4/4/2/4/3 and a 3/3/3/3/3 have the same
mean and are different hires. The rubrics name which competencies are hard stops
per role — market insight for the GTM lead, adaptability and commercial judgment
for the CSM.

**Score coherence across stages.** For the GTM assessment, whether stage 2's
allocation follows from stage 1's diagnosis is worth more than the quality of
either alone. Plenty of people can produce a sharp analysis and an unrelated
plan.

**Two blind reviewers, reconcile gaps of 2+.** Single-reviewer scoring drifts.
The reconciliation conversation is also the fastest way to find a rubric anchor
that is not doing its job.

## Legal and validity constraints

These are not optional and they shape the design.

**Job-relatedness.** Under Title VII, an assessment that produces adverse impact
must be demonstrably job-related and consistent with business necessity. Every
competency here maps to a task the role performs. Do not add a stage because it
is interesting.

**Same scenario, same time, same rubric for every candidate at a level.** The
consistency is the defensibility. Ad-hoc variation per candidate destroys both
the comparison and the legal position.

**Structured scoring beats holistic impression.** This is both the
best-established finding in the selection literature and the thing that makes an
audit survivable.

**Keep the records.** Responses, scores, reviewer identity, date. NYC Local Law
144 requires annual bias audits for automated employment decision tools and
candidate notice; Illinois and Maryland have their own requirements. If you
later add any automated scoring, check current obligations in your jurisdictions
before it goes live — the rules have been moving quickly.

**Watch adverse impact once you have volume.** Track pass rates by demographic
group. The four-fifths rule is the conventional screen. A structured assessment
is not automatically a fair one; it is only auditable.

**Pay for long assessments.** These run 45-50 minutes, which is generally
accepted as unpaid. Anything that becomes multi-hour work should be paid, and in
some jurisdictions must be.

## What this deliberately does not do

- **No facial, vocal, or affect analysis.** No validated link to job
  performance, prohibited in employment contexts under the EU AI Act, and it
  reliably encodes demographic proxies.
- **No personality inference.** Weak predictive validity for job performance
  compared to work samples, and poor legal footing.
- **No automated pass/fail.** A human reviewer makes the call. Automated scoring
  triggers a body of regulation these assessments are designed to stay clear of.
- **No timed-pressure gimmicks.** Time limits here are generous enough to be
  realistic and exist to bound effort, not to induce stress. Stress performance
  is a different construct and mostly measures something you are not hiring for.
