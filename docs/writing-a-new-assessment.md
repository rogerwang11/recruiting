# Adding a role to the series

Roughly 4-6 hours of work per assessment, most of it in step 2 and step 5.

## 1. Name 5 competencies (30 min)

Three of the five are shared across every role in the series and should stay
fixed: `diagnostic_rigor`, `adaptability`, `written_communication`. That shared
spine is what lets you compare a CSM candidate to a GTM candidate on the
dimensions that generalize.

The remaining two are role-specific. Pick them by asking what a strong performer
does in the first 90 days that a weak one does not. For the CSM those were
prioritization under constraint and commercial judgment. For the GTM lead,
resource allocation and market insight.

Write a one-sentence definition for each that describes observable behavior. If
you cannot describe what doing it badly looks like, it is not a competency, it
is a value.

## 2. Build the scenario (2 hours)

Start from a real situation someone in the role actually faced. Fiction invented
from scratch reads as fiction and candidates disengage.

Then apply the design rules from `design-principles.md`:

- One buried binding constraint, derivable but not stated
- The loudest signal is a symptom, not the cause
- A causal chain connecting three or four of the facts, so the diagnosis rewards
  synthesis rather than recall
- Enough irrelevant detail that filtering is part of the task

Sizing: 9-11 facts in stage 1. Fewer and there is nothing to filter. More and
you are testing reading stamina.

## 3. Cost the stage 2 menu (45 min)

8-10 options. Requirements:

- One that exceeds the budget outright
- One cheap information-gathering action that should come first
- One expensive artifact that feels productive and is premature
- One reflex answer that is wrong for this situation (the discount, the extra
  headcount)
- At least two that are genuinely reasonable, so it is not a single-answer test

## 4. Write the curveball (45 min)

The test: does the new information change the *category* of the problem, or just
its difficulty? If a candidate's stage 2 plan is still roughly correct after the
curveball, the curveball is too weak.

Make sure at least one element of the original plan should survive. A curveball
where everything changes rewards over-rotating, and you want to distinguish
adaptation from panic.

## 5. Write the rubric (1.5 hours)

This takes longer than writing the scenario and matters more.

For each competency, write four anchors describing what that level actually
looks like *in this scenario*. Generic anchors produce inconsistent scoring.

The reliable way to write them: draft the level 4 answer yourself, then the level
1 answer, then fill the middle. If you cannot write a convincing level 1, the
stage has no failure mode and will not discriminate.

## 6. Calibrate before using it (1 hour, once)

Have 2-3 people currently doing the job well take the assessment. Score them.

- If they all score 4s, it is too easy or the rubric is too generous.
- If a strong performer scores 2s, the rubric is measuring something other than
  the job.
- If two reviewers disagree by 2+ levels on the same answer, rewrite that anchor.

This step is skipped constantly and it is the one that determines whether the
assessment predicts anything.

## 7. Record the bar

State the mean threshold and which competencies are hard stops for the role.
Write it down before the first real candidate, not after — a bar set once you
have seen the candidates is not a bar.
